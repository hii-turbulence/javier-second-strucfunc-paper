> In the first paragraph of Section 3.1 you say that pixels associated with the high velocity objects were deleted. That is certainly the right thing to have done. However, there are many velocity variations that are due to the underlying structure of the main ionization front. The most vivid example is along the Bright Bar. In this case it is clear that data there should not be used in a statistical analysis. There are numerous studies that show often one sees edge-on regions, a good example being the brightest part of the nebula that lies to the SW from Theta1OriC, on the NE edge of the Orion South foreground cloud.  One can extend this argument to question whether such a detailed multi-parameter analysis using an assumption of turbulence is worth doing. This justification needs to be made in the paper.
>

The referee notes that projection effects associated with the ionization front significantly affect the observed velocity field. We recognize that this issue requires clarification in the manuscript. The aim of the present study is not to isolate an idealized, homogeneous turbulent cascade. Rather, it is to characterize the spatial statistics of the measured velocity field in the ionized gas. These statistics inevitably incorporate contributions from photoevaporative flows, ionization-front geometry, other large-scale ordered motions, and genuine turbulent fluctuations.

The complex structure of the ionization front as the H II region expands into a highly inhomogeneous medium contributes to the observed velocity fluctuations whose spatial statistics are examined here. As a result, attempting to exclude edge-on regions such as the Bright Bar would be counterproductive, even if it were possible (as the referee correctly notes, many such regions exist).

The physical origin of these velocity fluctuations, and the relative contributions of photoevaporative flows and turbulence, were examined in detail in earlier papers (Medina et al. 2014, MNRAS, 445, 1797; Arthur et al. 2016, MNRAS, 463, 2864), where empirical results were compared with synthetic observations from numerical simulations. See especially Fig. 16 of Arthur et al. (2016), which summarizes the conceptual scheme of causal relationships. The present paper has a narrower goal: to assess the methodology used to retrieve the characteristic fluctuation amplitude, correlation length, and power-law slope from observed structure functions.

The introduction has been revised to clarify these points and to emphasize that the term “turbulence” is employed as a shorthand for all globally disordered and chaotic motions within the nebula, even though these motions do not necessarily represent homogeneous hydrodynamic turbulence in the Kolmogorov sense, and even though a particular portion of the flow may exhibit a relatively ordered structure when examined at higher resolution.

> I certainly agree with the criticism of the Anorve-Zeferino papers and of course a basic premise of this paper is a comparison with the McLeod work. However, I believe that some quantitative statements need to be made about the early studies of [OIII] (Castaneda 1988, ApJSS, 67, 93), [OI] (O'Dell and Wen 1992, ApJ, 387, 229), and [SIII] (Wen 1993, ApJ, 409, 262). These studies have the obvious disadvantage of incomplete mapping, but were made at twice the velocity resolution of the later KPNO studies.

This is very true. Although a detailed comparison with those works was presented in Table 5 of Arthur et al. (2016), it is important to include a comparison in this paper too. We have therefore added a new Section 5.4.3 comparing our derived parameters with the earlier [O III], [O I], and [S III] studies.
